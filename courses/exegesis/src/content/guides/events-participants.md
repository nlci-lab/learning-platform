A sentence tells about **events**: things that happen or that someone does. Each event has **participants**: the people or things that take part in it. A translator must find every event in the source and know who takes part in it. A checker must find out whether the draft names the right participants.

This is easy when the source uses a verb with a subject: "Zacchaeus came down". It is harder when the event hides in a noun, in a weak verb or in a passive verb. This guide shows how to find it in each case.

## Two sets of names

As in [How clauses relate](~/guides/clause-relations/), we give the plain name first and the technical label from books on [semantic structure](~/glossary/#semantic-structure) after it.

Those books sort concepts into four classes, remembered as **[TEAR](~/glossary/#tear)**: Thing, Event, Attribute and Relation. An **[event](~/glossary/#event-idea)** is an action, a process or a happening. They also name the part each participant plays in an event. The two you need most are:

- **[Agent](~/glossary/#agent)**: the one who does the action. In "the servants gave him bread", the servants are the agent.
- **[Patient](~/glossary/#patient)**, also called **affected**: the one the action is done to, or that changes. In the same sentence, the bread is the patient.

Other roles include the one who receives something (Jeremiah, in the same sentence), the thing used to do the action, and the place. You will meet more labels in handbooks and consultants' notes; they all answer the question "what part does this person or thing play in the event?"

## Events hidden in nouns

Greek, Hebrew and English often name an event with a noun: "salvation", "forgiveness", "repentance". These are **[abstract nouns](~/glossary/#abstract-noun)**. Many languages say the same idea with a verb. When you change the noun to a verb, you must also say who does it and to whom.

"Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9, ULT). "Salvation" is the event *save*. To say it with a verb, you must answer two questions. Who is saved? The people of the house. Who saves? The verse does not say. A translator must find the answer in the context, or choose a form of words that does not need it, such as "Today the people of this house have been saved". If the language has no such form, the translator must name the agent. That is a question about meaning, not just grammar, so take it to the context and the commentaries.

A useful step: underline every noun in a verse that names an event, and write each one as a verb with its participants. You will often find a participant the source leaves unstated.

## Every event has participants

The author does not always name every participant. The first readers knew who was meant. Our readers may not. This is **[implicit information](~/glossary/#implicit-information)**: meaning the author expected the hearers to understand without saying it.

When a draft must name a participant the source leaves unstated, check two things:

- Is the participant clear from the context? If so, naming it may help the reader.
- Does naming it add meaning the author did not intend? If the context leaves it open, a draft that names one person has made a choice the text did not make.

## Weak verbs that hide the event

Some verbs carry little meaning on their own. The real event is in the noun that follows: "give an answer" means *answer*, "make a request" means *ask*. We call these **[empty verbs](~/glossary/#empty-verb)**. In "he made haste, and came down" (Luke 19:6, KJV), the event is *hurry*, not *make*.

A literal draft may keep the weak verb and the noun. In many languages this sounds strange, or it does not say what happened. Find the real event, then say it in the natural way.

## Passive verbs: who did it?

In an **active** sentence the subject does the action: "The king's servants gave him bread." In a **[passive](~/glossary/#passive)** sentence the subject is the one the action is done to: "A loaf of bread was given him every day" (Jeremiah 37:21b, ULT). The passive often does not say who acted.

An author may use the passive for several reasons:

- to keep the attention on the one the action is done to;
- because the one who acted is not known. In "the altar of Baal was torn down" (Judges 6:28a, ULT), the men of the town do not yet know who did it;
- because the author chooses not to name the one who acted.

Many languages have no passive, or use it for other reasons. A translator may then have three choices:

1. Use an active verb and name the agent: "The king's servants gave Jeremiah bread."
2. Use an active verb with a general subject, such as "people" or "someone".
3. Use a different verb whose subject is the patient: "Jeremiah received bread."

Each choice changes something. The first may add a participant the author did not name. The second may make the reader think of the wrong people. The third keeps the focus but may lose some meaning.

**When the one who acts may be God.** In some passive verbs, the context suggests that God is the one who acts, and the author does not name him. Readers and scholars disagree on how often this is so. This guide does not decide those cases. It only teaches the check: ask "who did it?" If the answer could be God, a vague "they" may mislead the reader into thinking of people. But naming God where the author may not mean him adds meaning. When the context leaves it open, treat it as a passage with more than one meaning: see [When a passage can mean more than one thing](~/guides/more-than-one-meaning/).

## Checking a draft

Use these questions with the translator, or with someone who reads the [back translation](~/glossary/#back-translation):

- **The event:** "What happened here?" If the reader names a thing instead of an action, the event may be lost.
- **The agent:** "Who did this?" Then: "How do you know?" If the answer comes from the draft but not from the source, the draft may have added a participant.
- **The patient:** "Who or what was this done to?"
- **A general subject:** if the draft says "they" or "people", ask "Who are 'they'?" If the reader names the wrong group, the draft needs a change.

Look for these common errors:

- an event noun translated as a thing, so the reader does not see an action;
- the wrong agent supplied, for example the nearest person in the sentence;
- a vague "they" that makes readers think of the wrong people;
- an agent named where the source leaves the agent open;
- an empty verb kept word for word, so the reader misses the real event.

**An example.** This draft and its back translation are invented for teaching. Source: "Today salvation has come to this house" (Luke 19:9, ULT). The back translation of a draft reads: "Today they have saved this house." Ask "Who are 'they'?" A reader says: "Jesus and his disciples." The source does not say that. The draft has also lost the people: "this house" sounds like a building. A better draft might say "Today the people of this family have been saved", or name an agent if the language must, and the team should agree that choice with the consultant.

## Where the text does not say

Sometimes the context does not show who acted, and interpreters disagree. Then the question is no longer how to find the participant, but which reading to choose. Weigh the options as in [When a passage can mean more than one thing](~/guides/more-than-one-meaning/), and write down the team's decision.
