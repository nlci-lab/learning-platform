A sentence is more than a list of events. The events are linked: one happens **after** another, one **causes** another, one is done **so that** another will happen. These links are **[relations between clauses](~/glossary/#clause-relation)**. A translator must see them in the source and show them in the new language. A checker must find out whether the draft kept them.

The basic course showed how to write relation words into a restatement, such as "because" or "in order that". This guide goes a step further. It gives each relation a name, so that you can say exactly what a draft got wrong.

## Two sets of names

We use the plain names from unfoldingWord **Translation Academy** (tA). Books on **semantic structure** (the meaning of a text set out as linked statements) use more technical labels. Consultants' notes, handbooks and articles use them, so you need to know both. Below, each relation has its plain name first and the technical label after it, where the two differ.

We sort the relations into three groups:

- **Time:** when the events happen in relation to each other.
- **Logic:** how one event leads to, depends on or goes against another.
- **Support:** one part helps another part without moving the thought forward.

## Time

- **[Sequential time](~/glossary/#sequential-time).** One event happens, then the next. "After I ate it, my stomach became bitter" (Revelation 10:10, ULT). Watch the order: a sentence with "before" may tell the later event first.
- **[Simultaneous time](~/glossary/#simultaneous-time).** Events happen at the same time, and neither causes the other: "While they were looking intensely into heaven as he was going up, suddenly, two men stood by them" (Acts 1:10, ULT).
- **[Background](~/glossary/#background-relation).** An ongoing situation sets the time for a main event that starts during it. The background is less important than the event it introduces.

## Logic

- **[Reason and result](~/glossary/#reason-result)** (reason–result, cause–effect). One event causes another. "A great storm arose on the sea, so that the boat was covered with the waves" (Matthew 8:24, ULT). Here "so that" shows a result, not a purpose: the storm did not intend anything.
- **[Goal, or purpose](~/glossary/#goal-purpose)** (means–purpose). Someone acts **intending** a second event. Gideon "was threshing out wheat at the winepress in order to hide from the presence of Midian" (Judges 6:11, ULT). The purpose may or may not be reached. English "so that" can mark both purpose and result, so check which one the source means.
- **[Means and result](~/glossary/#means-result)** (means–result). One action is the **way** another is achieved. Greek often states the means with a participle and no connecting word.
- **[Grounds and conclusion](~/glossary/#grounds-conclusion)** (grounds–conclusion). A fact is stated, and the speaker draws a conclusion from it. The fact does not cause the conclusion; it is the evidence for it.
- **Condition.** A **[hypothetical condition](~/glossary/#hypothetical-condition)** says that one event happens only if another does (condition–consequence). A **[factual condition](~/glossary/#factual-condition)** has the form "if", but the speaker treats it as true. A **[contrary-to-fact condition](~/glossary/#contrary-to-fact-condition)** has the form "if", but the speaker knows it is false. Some languages must turn a factual condition into "since", or readers will think the speaker is in doubt.
- **[Contrast](~/glossary/#contrast-relation).** Two things stand against each other: "Yet I am among you as one who serves" (Luke 22:27, ULT). The technical books separate simple **contrast** from **[concession–contraexpectation](~/glossary/#concession-contraexpectation)**: "although X, Y", where X makes us expect the opposite of Y.
- **[Exception](~/glossary/#exception-relation).** A part is taken out of a group: "no one ... except", "not ... unless". In some languages this sounds like a contradiction and is better said with "only".

## Support

Some parts do not add a new step. They help another part. These are **[support relations](~/glossary/#support-relation)**. The technical labels are **restatement** (saying it again in other words), **amplification** (adding detail), **clarification** (making it plainer), **comment** (the speaker's remark on it) and **comparison** (showing what it is like).

## Head and support

In every linked pair, one part usually carries the **main point**. It is the **[head](~/glossary/#head-proposition)**. The other part is the **[support](~/glossary/#support-proposition)**: it gives the reason, the means, the purpose, the time or other help. In "He went to the market to buy rice", the going is the head and buying rice is the purpose.

The head is not always the first clause. Greek often puts the support in a participle and the head in the main verb. A draft can go wrong by turning a support part into a new main event. Then the reader hears two equal events where the author meant one event and its reason.

## Checking a draft

When you check, do not only ask "Is every word there?" Ask how the parts link. These questions help, with the translator or with a reader of the back translation:

- **Time:** "What happened first? What happened next?" "Were these happening at the same time?"
- **Reason and result:** "Why did this happen?" "What happened because of it?"
- **Goal:** "Why did he do this? What did he want to happen?" Then: "Did it happen?" If the reader says it surely happened, the draft may have turned a purpose into a result.
- **Condition:** "Is the speaker sure about this, or not sure?"
- **Contrast:** "Was this what people expected?"
- **Head:** "What is the main thing this verse says?" If the reader names a support part, the draft may have changed the main point.

Common errors are:

- a reason and its result in an order the language does not allow;
- a purpose rendered as a plain "and";
- a result heard as a purpose;
- a factual condition heard as a doubt;
- a support clause made into a separate main sentence.

## Where the text does not say

Often the source gives no connecting word. Greek may join clauses with a participle or a simple "and" (*kai*), and the reader must find the relation from the context. A later guide in this unit, on links the text leaves unmarked, deals with that. When the context allows two relations and scholars disagree, weigh them as in [When a passage can mean more than one thing](~/guides/more-than-one-meaning/).
