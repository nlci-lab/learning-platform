The events in a sentence are linked. One happens **after** another, one **causes** another, one is done **so that** another will happen. These links are **[relations between clauses](~/glossary/#clause-relation)**. A translator must see them in the source and show them in the [receptor language](~/glossary/#receptor-language). A checker must find out whether the draft kept them.

You may already add relation words such as "because" or "in order that" when you restate a verse in plain words. This guide gives each relation a name, so that you can say exactly what a draft got wrong.

## Two sets of names

Where unfoldingWord **Translation Academy** (tA) has a plain name for a relation, we use it. Books on **semantic structure** use more technical labels. These books set out the meaning of a text as a set of simple statements and show how the statements link. You will meet their labels in consultants' notes, handbooks and articles, so you need to know both sets of names. Below, each relation has its plain name first. Where the technical label is different, it follows in brackets. A few relations have no tA name, so we use the technical one.

We sort the relations into three groups:

- **Time:** when the events happen in relation to each other.
- **Logic:** how one event causes another, depends on it, or is opposed to it.
- **Support:** one part helps another part without moving the thought forward.

## Time

- **[Sequential time](~/glossary/#sequential-time).** One event happens, then the next. "After I ate it, my stomach became bitter" (Revelation 10:10b, ULT). Watch the order: a sentence with "before" may tell the later event first.
- **[Simultaneous time](~/glossary/#simultaneous-time).** Events happen at the same time, and neither causes the other: "While they were looking intensely into heaven as he was going up, suddenly, two men stood by them …" (Acts 1:10, ULT).
- **[Background](~/glossary/#background-relation).** An ongoing situation gives the time or setting for a main event that starts during it. The main event is in focus.

## Logic

- **[Reason and result](~/glossary/#reason-result)** (reason–result, cause–effect). One event causes another. "A great storm arose on the sea, so that the boat was covered with the waves" (Matthew 8:24a, ULT). Here "so that" shows a result, not a purpose: the storm did not intend anything.
- **[Goal, or purpose](~/glossary/#goal-purpose)** (means–purpose: the action is the means, the intended event is the purpose). Someone acts **intending** a second event. Gideon "was threshing out wheat at the winepress in order to hide from the presence of Midian" (Judges 6:11b, ULT). English "so that" can mark both purpose and result, so check which one the source means.
- **[Means and result](~/glossary/#means-result).** One action is the **way** another is achieved. tA has no separate name for this. Greek may state the means with a **participle**: a verb form, like English "working", that adds an action to the main verb. A participle can carry several other relations too, such as time or reason.
- **[Grounds and conclusion](~/glossary/#grounds-conclusion).** A fact is stated, and the speaker draws a conclusion from it. The fact does not cause the conclusion; it is the evidence for it. When the grounds lead to a command, as often with "therefore" in the letters, the technical books call it **[grounds and exhortation](~/glossary/#grounds-exhortation)**.
- **Condition.** The technical books call all of these condition–consequence. tA splits them in three:
  - a **[hypothetical condition](~/glossary/#hypothetical-condition)**: if one event happens, another will follow;
  - a **[factual condition](~/glossary/#factual-condition)**: the form is "if", but the speaker treats it as true;
  - a **[contrary-to-fact condition](~/glossary/#contrary-to-fact-condition)**: the form is "if", but the speaker knows it is false.

  Some languages must turn a factual condition into "since". Otherwise readers will think the speaker is in doubt.
- **[Contrast](~/glossary/#contrast-relation).** Two things stand against each other: "Yet I am among you as one who serves" (Luke 22:27, ULT). The technical books split this in two. Simple **contrast** sets two things side by side. **[Concession–contraexpectation](~/glossary/#concession-contraexpectation)** means "although X, Y": X makes us expect the opposite of Y.
- **[Exception](~/glossary/#exception-relation).** A part is taken out of a group: "no one ... except", "not ... unless". In some languages this sounds like a contradiction, so it is better to use "only".

## Support

Some parts do not add a new step. They help another part. These are **[support relations](~/glossary/#support-relation)**. The technical labels include **restatement** (saying the same thing again in other words, for example general then specific), **amplification** (adding detail), **comment** (the speaker's remark on it), **comparison** (showing what it is like), **illustration** and **manner** (how something was done).

## Head and support

In many pairs of linked clauses, one part carries the **main point**. It is the **[head](~/glossary/#head-proposition)**. The other part is the **[support](~/glossary/#support-proposition)**: it gives the reason, the means, the time or something else that helps the head. In "He stayed at home because he was sick", staying at home is the head and being sick is the reason. In a pair of events told in sequence, or in a simple contrast, the two parts may be equal.

The head is not always the first clause. Greek often puts a support part in a participle and the head in the main verb, so the head may come later. A draft can go wrong by turning a support part into a new main event. If that happens, the reader hears two equal events where the author meant one event and its support.

## Checking a draft

Ask more than "Is every word there?" Ask how the parts link. Use these questions with the translator, or with someone who reads the [back translation](~/glossary/#back-translation):

- **Time:** "What happened first? What happened next?" "Were these happening at the same time?"
- **Reason and result:** "Why did this happen?" "What happened because of it?"
- **Goal:** "Why did he do this? What did he want to happen?" Then: "Did it happen?" If the reader answers that the draft says it happened, the draft may have turned a purpose into a result.
- **Condition:** "Is the speaker sure about this, or not sure?"
- **Contrast:** "How are these two things different?" For concession: "Was this what people expected?"
- **Head:** "What is the main thing this verse says?" If the reader names a support part, the draft may have changed the main point.

Look for these common errors:

- a reason and its result in an order the language does not allow;
- a purpose translated as a plain "and";
- a result understood as a purpose;
- a factual condition understood as a doubt;
- a support clause made into a separate main sentence.

## Where the text does not say

Often the source gives no connecting word. Greek may join clauses with a participle or a simple "and" (*kai*), and the reader must find the relation from the context. When the context allows two relations and scholars disagree, weigh them as in [When a passage can mean more than one thing](~/guides/more-than-one-meaning/).
