Use this method when a passage can be read in more than one way. It works for a translator preparing to draft and for a checker reviewing a draft.

## 1. List the readings

Write each possible reading as a plain sentence. Find them in:

- several Bible versions, close and meaning-based, in English and in the national language;
- translator's helps, such as the UBS Translator's Handbooks and SIL Translator's Notes (both in Paratext);
- commentaries and exegetical summaries.

If two versions say different things, there is usually a real question behind it.

## 2. Test each reading

Ask these five questions of every reading:

1. **Grammar.** Does the Greek or Hebrew allow it? Check the form, not only the English.
2. **The sentence and paragraph.** Does it fit what comes just before and just after?
3. **The book.** Does it fit the author's purpose and the themes of the whole book?
4. **The author's habits.** How does the same author use this word or phrase elsewhere?
5. **The first hearers.** Would the original audience, with their culture and history, have understood it this way?

A sixteenth-century translator, Miles Coverdale, put the same idea as a list: notice not only what is said, but "of whom, and to whom", with what words, when, where, why, and what comes before and after.

## 3. Weigh the results

A reading that fails a test is weak. A reading that passes all five is strong. Often one reading is clearly stronger. Sometimes two stay close.

## 4. Decide

- **One reading is clearly stronger:** translate it.
- **Two readings stay close:** translate the one that fits the context best, and give the other in a footnote.
- **The receptor language can naturally keep both open:** you may do so, with a footnote that explains.

Your choice must never contradict the theme of the passage or its immediate context.

## 5. Check the draft says it clearly

After the decision, read the draft again. It should say the chosen reading clearly, without mixing in the other.

## 6. Record the decision

Write a short note, in Paratext or in your checking report, so that others can see what you decided and why. Three or four lines are enough:

> **Luke 2:49.** Readings: "in my Father's house" / "about my Father's business". Chosen: house. Reasons: they find him in the temple (2:46); the parents' question is where he has been. Footnote: "Or *about my Father's business*."

A checker who reads this note can agree, or ask a question, without starting again from nothing.
