## Exegesis comes first

Exegesis means finding out what the original author meant. A translator does it before drafting; a checker does it again before judging the draft. Most of the time the meaning is clear. Sometimes it is not, and good readers disagree.

## Why the same words can be read in different ways

A passage can be open to more than one reading for several reasons:

- **We were not there.** The author wrote for people who shared a time, a place and a culture with him. We do not have the whole picture they had.
- **The original languages work differently.** Greek and Hebrew can leave things unsaid that our languages must say: who did something, how two ideas connect, whether a word means "that" or "because".
- **Words have more than one sense.** One word can cover several meanings, and readers may not agree which one the author had in mind here.
- **The grammar allows more than one link.** A phrase may attach to more than one part of the sentence.

## An example: Luke 2:49

When Mary and Joseph find the boy Jesus in the temple, he answers them. The Greek says, word for word, "in the [things] of my Father". It does not say *what* things.

- The NIV reads it as a place: "Didn't you know I had to be **in my Father's house**?"
- The King James Version reads it as activity: "wist ye not that I must be **about my Father's business**?"

Both readings fit the Greek. The setting (the temple, where they found him) gives weight to the first. A team has to choose.

## A translator has to choose

In many languages a translator cannot keep the vague wording of the original. "In the things of my Father" may mean nothing at all. So the team must study the options, choose the reading that fits best, and translate that.

There are three ways to handle it:

1. **Translate the stronger reading, and put the other one in a footnote.** This is the usual choice in a meaning-based translation when two readings are both reasonable.
2. **Translate the stronger reading with no footnote,** when the other reading is clearly weaker.
3. **Keep wording that is open to both,** when the receptor language allows it naturally, and explain in a footnote. This is more common in close, form-based translations.

## What a checker looks for

- Did the team **notice** that the passage has more than one reading?
- Did they **study** the options with the helps, or follow one version without thinking?
- Does their choice **fit the context**: the sentence, the paragraph, the book?
- If the other reading is strong, is there a **footnote**?
- Does the draft say one thing **clearly**, rather than a vague mix of both?
