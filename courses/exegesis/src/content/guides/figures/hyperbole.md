## What it is

[Hyperbole](~/glossary/#hyperbole) is a deliberate exaggeration. The speaker says more than is literally true to give emphasis or a strong effect. The hearer is not meant to take it literally (Barnwell, ch. 34.4).

When an English speaker says "I'm starving", the hearer knows the speaker is only very hungry. But Barnwell notes that some languages do not use this figure. In those languages a word-for-word hyperbole may be taken as a plain fact.

## How to spot it

- Words like "all", "every", "no one", "never" or "the whole world" appear where the context shows that this cannot be exact.
- Very large numbers appear in praise, poetry or a song.
- The statement conflicts with another verse or with the story itself.
- The speaker is excited, angry, afraid or praising someone.
- A literal reading would make the speaker say something false or absurd.

## Everyday examples

- "I have told you a thousand times." I have told you many times.
- "This bag weighs a ton." The bag is very heavy.
- "Everybody was at the wedding." Very many people came.
- "I nearly died laughing." I laughed very hard.

## Bible examples

**Matthew 11:18.** "For John came neither eating nor drinking" (KJV). John did eat: Mark 1:6 mentions locusts and wild honey. Barnwell explains that the point is that John ate simply, often fasted and drank no wine (ch. 34.4). The checker should notice whether readers could think John never ate at all.

**John 12:19.** The Pharisees say, "Look how the whole world has gone after him!" (NIV). They mean that very many people are following Jesus, and they feel helpless. The checker should notice that the emphasis carries the Pharisees' alarm.

**1 Samuel 18:7.** The women sing, "Saul hath slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands" (KJV). The numbers are not a count. They praise both men and give David more honour, which makes Saul angry in the next verse. The checker should notice whether the comparison between the two men stays clear.

**Numbers 13:27.** The spies say the land "it does flow with milk and honey" (NIV). This describes a very rich and fertile land, and it echoes God's promise in Exodus 3:8. Barnwell lists it as hyperbole (ch. 34.4). Because the phrase repeats across books, the team may want to keep it and explain it once.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | Direction | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperbole | Says more than is true | "the whole world has gone after him" |
| [Litotes](~/learn/figures/litotes/) | Says less, by denying the opposite | "no small stir" |
| [Irony](~/learn/figures/irony/) | Says the opposite of what is meant | "a fine way of rejecting God's command" |
| [Metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/) | Compares two things | "You are the light of the world" |

Hyperbole and litotes both give emphasis, but in opposite ways. Hyperbole overstates. Litotes understates. Both are lost if the translation simply states a weaker fact.

## How translations handle it

Barnwell says that where a language does not use hyperbole, the translator should express the actual meaning and keep the emphasis of the original (ch. 34.4). Options:

- **Keep the figure** if receptor-language speakers use the same kind of exaggeration and will not take it literally.
- **Say the meaning plainly, with emphasis:** "very many people", "John fasted often and never drank wine". Barnwell reports a rendering in one South Sudanese language that says John came fasting and not drinking strong drink.
- **Use a receptor-language exaggeration** that hearers know is not literal.
- **Add a footnote** for a repeated phrase such as "milk and honey".

The danger runs two ways. A literal hyperbole can give false information. A plain rendering can lose the force. Most English versions, the KJV and NIV included, keep the hyperboles above.

## For the checker

1. Is this statement meant to be exactly true? How do you know from the context?
2. When people hear this, will they believe it is literally true? Ask someone who did not help translate.
3. Does your language exaggerate like this? In what situations?
4. If the team removed the exaggeration, what word or form now carries the emphasis?
5. Does the translation still show the speaker's feeling, such as alarm, praise or anger?
6. Could a reader find a contradiction with another verse because of this wording?
