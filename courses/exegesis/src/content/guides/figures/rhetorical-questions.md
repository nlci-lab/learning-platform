## What it is

Questions can have different purposes. A **real question** asks for information and expects an answer. When Jesus asks "How many loaves have ye?" (Mark 6:38, KJV), the disciples answer: five, and two fish (Barnwell, ch. 35.1).

A **[rhetorical question](~/glossary/#rhetorical-question)** does not ask for information. It has some other purpose. The speaker does not expect an answer, or already knows it.

Barnwell lists six common functions of rhetorical questions in the Bible (ch. 35.3):

1. **To emphasise a fact that is obviously true.** "How can Satan cast out Satan?" (Mark 3:23, KJV) means: Satan cannot drive out Satan.
2. **To focus on a condition.** "Is any among you afflicted? let him pray" (James 5:13, KJV) means: if anyone is suffering, he should pray.
3. **To introduce a topic or catch attention.** "Seest thou this woman?" (Luke 7:44, KJV). Simon can see her. Jesus uses the question to turn attention back to her.
4. **To express surprise.** In Mark 6:2–3 the people of Nazareth ask a series of questions about where Jesus got his wisdom. They show surprise and also offence.
5. **To rebuke or exhort.** "Why are ye so fearful?" (Mark 4:40, KJV) is a rebuke, not a request for reasons.
6. **To express uncertainty.** "What shall I do?" (Luke 12:17, KJV). The rich man speaks to himself.

One question may have more than one function. There are also other functions (ch. 35.3).

## How to spot it

- **No answer follows, or the speaker gives the answer.**
- **The answer is obvious** to everyone in the scene.
- **A command or statement follows**, which shows what the question really meant (James 5:13).
- **A string of questions** often shows strong emotion (Mark 6:2–3).
- **The form can point to the answer, but context decides.** In questions like these, a negative form often expects "yes" and a positive form often expects "no" (ch. 35.3.1): "Am not I a Philistine?" (1 Samuel 17:8, KJV) means "I am certainly a Philistine"; "Am I a Jew?" (John 18:35, KJV) means "I am certainly not a Jew." This is not a fixed rule. Some positive questions expect agreement, and some questions are invitations or requests with no implied answer. Always read the context to find what the speaker meant.
- **A new speech-introducing clause** may mark a change from rhetorical to real questions, as in Genesis 20:9–10 (ch. 35.3.5).

## Everyday examples

Barnwell gives English examples of everyday rhetorical questions (ch. 35.2). Here are some of our own:

1. "Can you pass the salt?" A polite request, not a question about ability.
2. "Who do you think you are?" Anger, not a question about identity.
3. "Isn't this a lovely garden?" An expression of feeling.
4. "Why didn't I think of that?" Regret, spoken to oneself.
5. "Would you like to sit down?" An invitation.

## Bible examples

**Mark 8:36.** "For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?" (KJV). The meaning is a strong statement: there is no gain at all (ch. 35.1). *The checker should notice:* the hearer must not think Jesus is asking for an amount.

**1 Samuel 17:8 and John 18:35.** "Am not I a Philistine?" and "Am I a Jew?" (KJV). One is negative and implies yes. The other is positive and implies no (ch. 35.3.1). *The checker should notice:* in the receptor language, does each form imply the right answer?

**Luke 15:4.** Jesus asks which man with a hundred sheep would not leave the ninety-nine to look for the lost one. Barnwell reports a translation that used a "Do you think that..." form. In that language the question implied that no one would do such a foolish thing. The story that follows then made no sense (ch. 35.4). *The checker should notice:* the expected answer is "every one of you would".

**Genesis 18:15.** Sarah denies that she laughed. The answer is "Yes, you did laugh" (NIV). The Hebrew form is closer to "No, but you laughed" (ch. 35.7). *The checker should notice:* the words for "yes" and "no" work differently in different languages.

## Not to be confused with

| Kind | Asks for information? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Real question | Yes | "How many loaves have ye?" (Mark 6:38, KJV) |
| Rhetorical question | No; it states, rebukes, introduces and so on | "How can Satan cast out Satan?" (Mark 3:23, KJV) |
| [Irony](~/learn/figures/irony/) in question form | No; it means the opposite of its words | Doohyayo rendering of Mark 7:9 (see below) |

Real questions also carry attitudes. "Where on earth have you been?" expects an answer, but also shows surprise and irritation. "You won't be back by Tuesday, will you?" shows what the speaker expects (ch. 35.7). A good translation keeps these attitudes too.

## How translations handle it

Most languages have rhetorical questions, but not always with the same functions or as often (ch. 35.4). So a rhetorical question in the source text is not always best translated by a question. Options include:

- **Keep the question** where the receptor language uses it the same way.
- **Turn it into a statement, command or "if" clause.** Barnwell reports that the NLT often does this, as in 1 Samuel 6:6, where it uses a command and a statement in place of the questions (ch. 35.4). James 5:13 can become "If anyone is suffering, he should pray."
- **Make the implied answer explicit** where the receptor language needs it.
- **Add a rhetorical question** where the receptor language uses one and the source does not. Barnwell reports that Ebira (Nigeria) uses a question in Mark 10:25 to avoid an awkward construction (ch. 35.4).
- **Use a question for irony.** In Doohyayo (Cameroon), Mark 7:9 was rendered with a tag question to carry the irony (ch. 35.4).

Whatever form is used, keep the emphasis and the emotion. A flat statement may lose the speaker's anger or wonder.

**Answering negative questions.** Take the question "Aren't you coming?" If the person is not coming, English says "No". Many languages say "Yes", meaning "You are right, I am not coming". Barnwell reports this for Tok Pisin (ch. 35.7). The reverse is also true. Answers to statements differ too, as in Genesis 18:15. Check every answer to a negative question.

Translators should study how their own language uses questions: for what purposes, in what kinds of speech, and with what special words or particles (ch. 35.5).

## For the checker

1. Does the person asking this expect an answer? What is the answer?
2. What is the speaker doing with this question: making a point, rebuking, introducing a topic, showing surprise or uncertainty?
3. When people in your language hear this question, what answer do they think is expected?
4. In your language, would people use a question here, or a statement or command?
5. If we changed it to a statement, did we keep the strong feeling?
6. In this answer to a negative question, does "yes" or "no" mean what the text means?
