## What it is

A [figure of speech](~/glossary/#figure-of-speech) uses words in a way that is not meant literally. The words point to a meaning beyond what they say. "The whole town came to the meeting" does not mean every person was there. It means a great many came.

People use figures for good reasons:

- to make a point vivid and easy to remember;
- to add force or emphasis;
- to show the speaker's feelings, such as surprise, anger or sympathy;
- to say something gently that would sound harsh said plainly;
- to make the hearer think and take part in finding the meaning.

The Bible is full of figures, especially in poetry, prophecy and the teaching of Jesus. They are part of the message. A translation that loses them, or takes them literally, can lose the meaning or change it.

## Why a checker must notice them

A figure translated word for word can fail in three ways:

1. **No meaning.** The reader cannot see what it is about.
2. **Wrong meaning.** The reader takes it literally, or reads a different point into it.
3. **Lost force.** The meaning survives, but the emphasis or the feeling is gone.

The same figure may work in one language and fail in another. Every language has figures, but not the same ones, and not used in the same way.

## Three steps for every figure

1. **Spot it.** Notice that the words are not literal. Clues: something that cannot be literally true ("I am the door"), a sudden change of tone, an exaggeration, a question that expects no answer, a thing spoken of as a person.
2. **Find its meaning.** Work out what the author wanted the first hearers to understand, and what feeling or emphasis came with it. For a comparison, find the topic, the picture and the point they share.
3. **Judge the draft.** Does the translation give the same meaning, with the same force, in a natural way? See [Translating figures of speech](~/learn/figures/translating-figures/).

## Figures and idioms

An [idiom](~/learn/figures/idioms/) is a fixed phrase whose meaning is not the sum of its words: "kick the bucket" for "die". People use it without thinking of the picture. A figure of speech is a creative use of words: the speaker means the picture. Many idioms began as figures and became fixed over time. A checker treats both the same way at the start: find the real meaning, then see how the draft says it.

## Telling the common figures apart

| Figure | What it does | A quick test |
|---|---|---|
| [Metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/) | Compares two things without saying so | "X is Y", and X is not literally Y |
| [Simile](~/learn/figures/simile/) | Compares two things and says so | "like", "as", "than" |
| [Metonymy](~/learn/figures/metonymy/) | Names something closely linked to what is meant | "the sword" for fighting; no comparison |
| [Synecdoche](~/learn/figures/synecdoche/) | Names a part for the whole, or the whole for a part | "my tongue" for "I" |
| [Personification](~/learn/figures/personification/) | Speaks of a thing or an idea as a person | a thing that sings, sees or is ashamed |
| [Apostrophe](~/learn/figures/apostrophe/) | Speaks directly to a thing, or to someone absent | "O death, where is…" |
| [Euphemism](~/learn/figures/euphemism/) | Says something hard or rude in a mild way | "fell asleep" for "died" |
| [Hyperbole](~/learn/figures/hyperbole/) | Exaggerates for emphasis | cannot be literally true, and is not meant to be |
| [Litotes](~/learn/figures/litotes/) | Says something strongly by denying its opposite | "not a little" for "very much" |
| [Irony](~/learn/figures/irony/) | Says the opposite of what is meant, in pretended agreement | praise that is really blame |
| [Sarcasm](~/learn/figures/sarcasm/) | Mocks or rebukes, often with irony, in a cutting way | a taunt |
| [Rhetorical question](~/learn/figures/rhetorical-questions/) | A question that asks for no information | the answer is obvious, or none is wanted |

The names matter less than the meaning. A checker who can explain what a passage means, and why the author said it that way, has done the main work, even without the technical term.
