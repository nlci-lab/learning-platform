## What it is

A [simile](~/glossary/#simile) is a comparison that says openly that it is a comparison. It uses a word such as "like" or "as". "He ran like the wind" compares his running to the wind.

A simile has the same three parts as a [metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/) (Barnwell, ch. 33.1, 33.3):

- the **topic**: what is talked about (his running);
- the **image**: what it is compared to (the wind);
- the **point of similarity**: how they are alike (fast).

Because the simile says "like", readers usually know a comparison is being made. The danger is different. Readers may pick the wrong point of similarity.

## How to spot it

- Look for comparison words: "like", "as", "as ... as", "than", "resembles", "in the same way".
- In Hebrew poetry, look for a full comparison: "As the deer pants for water, so my soul pants for you".
- Check whether "like" introduces a real comparison or just an example. "Animals like goats" gives an example. "He is like a goat" compares.
- In a back translation, check that the comparison word is still there. If it has gone, the simile has become a metaphor or a plain statement.

## Everyday examples

- "Her hands were as cold as ice." (very cold)
- "He sleeps like a log." (deeply, without moving)
- "The news spread like fire in dry grass." (fast and wide)
- "She is as busy as a bee." (always working)

The same image can mean different things in different places. In one culture an owl stands for wisdom; in another it may stand for bad luck. When a team compares a person to an animal, ask what that animal means locally.

## Bible examples

**Isaiah 53:6.** "All we like sheep have gone astray" (KJV). The topic is the people. The image is sheep. The verse states the point: like sheep, the people wandered off. Where sheep are unknown, a team may need a local animal that wanders, or a short description.

**Matthew 28:3.** "His countenance was like lightning" (KJV). In English, lightning often suggests speed. Barnwell notes that here the point is brightness, as the context and Luke 24:4 show (ch. 33.5). The checker should ask what readers think the angel's face was like. If they say "fast", the point has been lost.

**Luke 17:6.** "If you have faith as small as a mustard seed" (NIV). Barnwell notes that the original leaves the point unstated: a mustard seed is very small, so even a little faith can do great things. The NIV, NLT and GNT all make the point explicit, in slightly different ways (ch. 33.4). The checker should ask whether readers know that mustard seed is tiny.

**Matthew 10:16.** "I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves" (KJV). Jesus compares the disciples to sheep among wolves. The point is that they will be weak and in danger among hostile people. The checker should see that both images are understood, and that "wolves" are not taken as real animals.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | Comparison word? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simile | Yes: "like", "as" | "Your word is like a lamp." |
| [Metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/) | No | "Your word is a lamp." |
| [Hyperbole](~/learn/figures/hyperbole/) | Sometimes, but the point is exaggeration | "as many as the stars of heaven" |
| [Idiom](~/learn/figures/idioms/) | Sometimes, but the meaning is fixed | "sleep like a log" |

Many fixed English similes are also idioms. Their meaning cannot be worked out from the words, so they need the same care as any idiom.

## How translations handle it

Barnwell's guidelines (ch. 33.7–33.9) apply to similes as well as metaphors:

- **Keep the image** when readers know it and see the right point.
- **Add the point of similarity** when readers might pick the wrong one. Barnwell reports that the GNT and NLT add size words in Luke 17:6.
- **Use a local image** with the same point, if it does not clash with the setting.
- **Describe an unknown image** briefly, or explain it in a footnote.
- **Keep the comparison word.** Without it, readers may take the sentence literally.

Do not add a point of similarity when the context already makes it clear. Too much explanation makes the text heavy and can narrow the meaning.

## For the checker

1. What is being compared here, and with what?
2. In what way are they alike? Ask a reader to explain it in their own words.
3. Do readers know the image? Does it mean the same thing in your culture?
4. Could readers choose a different point of similarity from the one the author meant?
5. Is the comparison word still in the translation and in the back translation?
6. Did you add anything to explain the comparison? Was it needed?
