## What it is

A [metaphor](~/glossary/#metaphor) is a comparison that does not say it is a comparison. The speaker says one thing *is* another thing. "My boss is a lion" does not mean the boss has fur. It means the boss is like a lion in some way, for example fierce or strong.

Every metaphor has three parts (Barnwell, ch. 33.3):

- the **topic**: what the speaker is talking about (the boss);
- the **image**: what the topic is compared to (a lion);
- the **point of similarity**: how the two are alike (fierce).

Often one of these parts is not stated. The hearer must supply it from the context or from shared background knowledge (Barnwell, ch. 33.4, 33.8). That is where problems in translation start.

## How to spot it

- The sentence says "X is Y", but X cannot literally be Y. A person is called an animal, a tool, a building or a plant.
- There is no "like" or "as". If there is, it is a [simile](~/learn/figures/simile/).
- A verb belongs to the image, not the topic. "He planted doubts in their minds" uses a farming verb for ideas.
- A word for an animal or object is used as a name or a form of address: "you snakes".
- In a back translation, the sentence reads as nonsense or as a strange fact. That often means the team kept a metaphor that the readers took literally.

## Everyday examples

- "The classroom was a zoo." (It was noisy and out of control.)
- "She has a heart of stone." (She feels no pity.)
- "He is the backbone of the family." (The family depends on him.)
- "Time is money." (Wasted time is a loss.)

Every language has its own metaphors. A translator can collect common ones in their own language and note which images people use for strength, danger, foolishness or kindness. These lists help when a team looks for a natural way to keep a biblical image.

## Bible examples

**Genesis 49:27.** "Benjamin is a ravenous wolf" (NIV). The topic is Benjamin (the tribe). The image is a hungry wolf. The point of similarity is not stated: Benjamin will be fierce and will destroy his enemies (Barnwell, ch. 33.3). The checker should ask whether readers know what wolves are like and whether they could think Benjamin turns into an animal.

**Psalm 119:105.** "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path" (KJV). God's word is compared to a lamp. The point is that it shows the right way to live, as a lamp shows a person where to walk at night. The point is clear in most cultures, so the image can usually stay.

**John 10:11.** "I am the good shepherd" (NIV). Jesus compares himself to a shepherd. The verse goes on to say the good shepherd gives his life for the sheep, so the context states the point. The checker should notice whether "shepherd" is a known role. If people do not keep sheep, the team may need a word for someone who cares for animals.

**Mark 1:17.** "I will make you to become fishers of men" (KJV). Here the point of similarity is itself figurative: bringing people to follow Jesus is like catching fish (Barnwell, ch. 33.6). The NIV says "fish for people". Barnwell reports that the CEV states that the disciples will bring in people instead of fish. The checker should ask what the readers think "catching people" means. In some places it could sound like kidnapping.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | How it compares | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Metaphor | Says X *is* Y, no comparison word | "He is a rock." |
| [Simile](~/learn/figures/simile/) | Says X is *like* Y | "He is like a rock." |
| [Metonymy](~/learn/figures/metonymy/) | Uses a related word, not a comparison | "The pen is mightier than the sword." |
| [Personification](~/learn/figures/personification/) | Speaks of a thing as a person | "The trees clapped their hands." |

The test is this: if you can say "the topic is like the image because both are ...", it is a comparison. Metonymy is about association, not likeness.

## How translations handle it

Barnwell gives these guidelines (ch. 33.7–33.9):

- **Keep the image** when readers will understand it.
- **Turn it into a simile** so readers know it is a comparison. For Genesis 49:27, Barnwell reports that the GNT says Benjamin is *like* a vicious wolf.
- **Add the point of similarity** when readers cannot supply it.
- **Use a different image** that carries the same meaning in the receptor culture.
- **Drop the image and state the meaning.** For Matthew 5:6, Barnwell reports that the GNT drops "hunger and thirst" and speaks of people whose greatest desire is to do what God requires.
- **Add a footnote** to explain an unknown image.

Barnwell also warns that stating the point can weaken the figure or narrow it. A metaphor may have more than one point of similarity (ch. 33.9). Keep the image where you can.

## For the checker

1. What or who is being talked about here, and what is it compared to?
2. In what way are the two alike? Can someone from the community tell you without help?
3. Could a reader take this literally? What would they think happened?
4. Is the image known in your area? If not, what did you do about it?
5. Did you keep the image, make it a simile or state the meaning? Why?
6. Does the same image appear elsewhere in Scripture, and did you translate it the same way there?
