## What it is

A [synecdoche](~/glossary/#synecdoche) uses a part of something to refer to the whole, or the whole to refer to a part. In "all hands on deck", hands means sailors. In "Brazil won the match", the whole country stands for its team. Barnwell gives both kinds (ch. 34.6).

Synecdoche is a close relative of [metonymy](~/learn/figures/metonymy/). Both use one idea to refer to a related idea. In synecdoche the link is part and whole. In metonymy it can be any close association.

## How to spot it

- A body part does an action that the whole person does: a tongue rejoices, feet hurry to do evil, lips honour.
- A place name does something only people can do: a city "goes out" to someone.
- A wide word such as "flesh" or "the world" refers to people.
- A single item stands for a larger group of things: "bread" for food in general.
- The verb fits a person better than the noun in front of it.

## Everyday examples

- "We have three new faces in the office." Faces means people.
- "He finally bought his own wheels." Wheels means a car.
- "The school is going on a trip." The school means its pupils and teachers.
- "She earns her bread by sewing." Bread means a living, food and other needs.

## Bible examples

**Acts 2:26.** "My tongue rejoices" (NIV). The tongue stands for the speaker himself. Barnwell adds that it also shows the joy was spoken aloud, as praise in words (ch. 34.6). The checker should notice whether the translation sounds as though one body part was happy on its own.

**Luke 3:6.** "And all flesh shall see the salvation of God" (KJV). "Flesh" refers to human beings, and the NIV says "people". A word-for-word rendering may mean meat or bodies.

**Romans 3:15.** "Their feet are swift to shed blood" (KJV). The feet stand for the people, and their speed shows how eager they are to kill. "Shed blood" is itself a figure for killing. The checker should notice that there are two figures in one short line.

**Matthew 3:5.** "Then went out to him Jerusalem, and all Judaea" (KJV). The places stand for the people living there. The NIV says that people went out from these places. Barnwell lists this verse among her metonymy and synecdoche exercises (ch. 34.6, exercise 7).

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | Link between the two ideas | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Synecdoche | Part for whole, or whole for part | "tongue" for the person |
| [Metonymy](~/learn/figures/metonymy/) | Any close association | "throne" for royal rule |
| [Metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/) | A comparison | "Go tell that fox" |
| [Hyperbole](~/learn/figures/hyperbole/) | Deliberate exaggeration | "the whole world has gone after him" |

"All Judaea" in Matthew 3:5 can be read both as synecdoche (place for people) and as [hyperbole](~/learn/figures/hyperbole/) (not every single person went). A checker does not need to choose a label. The question is whether the translation gives the right meaning and force.

## How translations handle it

Barnwell says the actual meaning should be translated in a clear and natural way, because a word-for-word rendering can be nonsense (ch. 34.6). Options:

- **Keep the figure** if the receptor language uses the same part for the whole. Many languages use body parts this way, but each uses them differently.
- **Say the meaning plainly:** "I rejoice and praise God"; "all people will see".
- **Use a receptor-language synecdoche** with the same meaning, if one exists and sounds natural in that kind of text.
- **Combine:** keep the image and make the person explicit, for example "people from Jerusalem".
- **Add a footnote** where a poetic image matters, as in quoted Psalms.

The KJV usually keeps the figure. The NIV keeps it in Acts 2:26 and Romans 3:15, and says the meaning in Luke 3:6 and Matthew 3:5.

## For the checker

1. Who or what is really doing the action in this verse?
2. When people hear "tongue" (or "flesh", "feet") here, what do they understand?
3. Does your language use this part of the body to mean the whole person? In what kind of speech?
4. If the team made the meaning plain, did it lose any sense of feeling or emphasis?
5. Are there two figures in this line? Did the team handle both?
6. Does the back translation show the part, the whole, or both?
