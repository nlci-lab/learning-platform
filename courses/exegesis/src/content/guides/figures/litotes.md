## What it is

[Litotes](~/glossary/#litotes) is a strong statement made by saying that the opposite is not true. "Not a little" means "very much". "No ordinary city" means "an important city". Barnwell says the purpose is usually emphasis (ch. 34.3).

Litotes looks like understatement, because the words sound weak. But speakers who use it know that the meaning is strong.

## How to spot it

- A negative word ("not", "no") is joined to a word of small size or amount: "not a little", "no small", "not a few".
- A negative is joined to a word with a bad or weak meaning: "not disobedient", "no mean city".
- The negative statement seems too obvious to be worth saying, unless it is meant as emphasis.
- The story shows a strong result: a riot, great comfort, many believers.
- Acts uses this form often. Most of Barnwell's examples come from Acts (ch. 34.3).

## Everyday examples

- "That was not a bad meal." The meal was good, perhaps very good.
- "She is no fool." She is clever.
- "It is not cheap." It is expensive.
- "He was not unhappy with the result." He was pleased.

## Bible examples

**Acts 20:12.** After Eutychus is brought back alive, the people "were not a little comforted" (KJV). Barnwell reports that the NIV says they were "greatly comforted" (ch. 34.3). The checker should notice whether a literal rendering sounds as if they were only a bit comforted.

**Acts 21:39.** Paul says he is "a citizen of no mean city" (KJV). He means that Tarsus is an important city. In modern English "mean" can sound like "unkind". The NIV says "no ordinary city". The checker should notice whether the translation shows Paul's pride in his city.

**Acts 19:23.** "There arose no small stir about that way" (KJV). This means there was a great disturbance, and the story goes on to a riot in Ephesus. The checker should notice whether the size of the trouble comes through.

**Mark 12:34.** Jesus tells a teacher of the law, "You are not far from the kingdom of God" (NIV). The sense is "you are close". This one is milder than the examples from Acts. The checker should notice whether a literal rendering sounds like a rebuke ("you are still outside") instead of encouragement.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Litotes | Deny the opposite, for emphasis | "no small stir" |
| [Hyperbole](~/learn/figures/hyperbole/) | Overstate, for emphasis | "the whole world has gone after him" |
| [Irony](~/learn/figures/irony/) | Say the opposite of what is meant, often to mock | "a fine way of rejecting God's command" |
| [Euphemism](~/learn/figures/euphemism/) | Soften, to avoid offence | "fell asleep" for died |

Litotes and hyperbole are opposite tools with the same aim: emphasis. Irony also involves opposites, but its aim is to mock or rebuke. Litotes means what it implies, in a positive sense.

## How translations handle it

Barnwell says that some languages may need an emphatic positive form instead of a negative (ch. 34.3). His exercise asks translators to re-express litotes directly and to be careful to keep the emphasis. Options:

- **Keep the figure** if the receptor language uses litotes and hearers will understand it as strong.
- **Say it as a strong positive:** "greatly comforted", "a very great disturbance", "an important city".
- **Use a receptor-language form of emphasis**, such as an intensifier, repetition or an emphatic particle.
- **Footnotes** are rarely needed for litotes. A clear positive form usually works.

The common error is a weak positive: "they were comforted" or "there was a disturbance". The meaning is right, but the emphasis is gone. The KJV keeps the litotes. The NIV sometimes keeps it and sometimes changes it to a strong positive.

## For the checker

1. What does this phrase mean in plain words: a little, or very much?
2. When people hear this negative phrase, do they understand it as strong or as weak?
3. Does your language say things this way? Can you give an example from daily speech?
4. If the team changed it to a positive form, which word now carries the emphasis?
5. Does the strength of this phrase match what happens next in the story?
6. Compare the back translation with the source: was any emphasis lost or added?
