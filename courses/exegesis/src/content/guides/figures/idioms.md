## What it is

An **[idiom](~/glossary/#idiom)** is an expression in which the words together mean something different from the meanings of the separate words (Barnwell, ch. 4.1). English examples are "to be caught red-handed" and "it's raining cats and dogs". Idioms are often unique to one language and culture.

This page is in the figures series for contrast. An idiom is not really a [figure of speech](~/learn/figures/what-is-a-figure/). A figure is usually used creatively, for an effect such as emphasis or vividness. An idiom is a fixed expression. Speakers use it as one unit, often without thinking of the picture in its words.

A **dead metaphor** is a phrase that once was a [metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/) but no longer carries a comparison. It has become fixed through common use. Barnwell calls a dead metaphor a kind of idiom (ch. 33.10). Examples are "the leg of a table" and "the key to the problem".

## How to spot it

- **The literal meaning does not make sense** in the context, or makes the wrong sense.
- **The expression is fixed.** You cannot change its words without losing the meaning. "Raining dogs and cats" sounds wrong.
- **The same phrase appears in many places** with the same meaning.
- **Body parts often appear**: heart, hand, head, eyes, ears.
- **Versions differ widely in form** but agree in meaning. That often shows an idiom underneath.

## Everyday examples

1. "He kicked the bucket" means he died.
2. "She has a big heart" means she is generous.
3. "Break a leg!" is a wish of good luck before a performance.
4. "It costs an arm and a leg" means it is very expensive.

Translated word for word, each of these would confuse or mislead a speaker of another language.

## Bible examples

**Luke 2:51.** "His mother kept all these sayings in her heart" (KJV). It means she kept thinking about these things. Barnwell reports that in Kilba (Nigeria) the literal phrase means to bear a grudge. So the translator said directly that she went on thinking about them (ch. 4.2). *The checker should notice:* a literal idiom can carry a wrong meaning, not only an unclear one.

**Genesis 35:29.** Isaac died "old and full of days" (KJV). The NIV has "old and full of years". Barnwell reports that other English versions use a modern idiom about a ripe old age, or say that he had lived a full life (ch. 4.2). *The checker should notice:* the meaning is a long, complete life. It is not about counting days.

**Acts 18:6.** "Your blood be upon your own heads; I am clean" (KJV). Paul means they are responsible for their own fate, and he is not to blame. Barnwell reports that the GNT uses a natural English idiom about taking the blame, and that Igede (Nigeria) uses its own idiom about heads and hands (ch. 4.2). *The checker should notice:* "blood" here is not about wounds, and "clean" is not about washing.

**Acts 2:37.** The hearers "were pricked in their heart" (KJV); the NIV has "cut to the heart". Both mean they were deeply troubled. Barnwell lists this verse as an idiom to analyse (ch. 4, exercise 4). *The checker should notice:* in some languages a literal version may suggest a physical injury.

## Not to be confused with

| | Idiom | [Metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/) | [Euphemism](~/learn/figures/euphemism/) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed or creative? | Fixed | Usually creative | Often fixed |
| Comparison intended? | No | Yes | No |
| Purpose | The ordinary way to say it | Illustrate, catch attention | Avoid offence |

A living metaphor asks the hearer to see a point of similarity. An idiom or dead metaphor does not. A dead metaphor can be translated as an idiom, with no need to keep a comparison (ch. 33.10). Many euphemisms are also idioms, such as "fell asleep" for "died" (ch. 34.2). [Metonymy](~/learn/figures/metonymy/) and [synecdoche](~/learn/figures/synecdoche/) may also work as idioms when they are in common use (ch. 34.6).

## How translations handle it

Usually an idiom cannot be translated literally. Translate it as a unit (ch. 4.2). Barnwell gives two ways:

- **Translate the meaning directly**, without an idiom. Barnwell reports that the NLT and CEV do this for "fruit of the womb" in Isaiah 13:18, speaking plainly of babies and children (ch. 4.2).
- **Use a receptor-language idiom with the same meaning.** It will usually be a different idiom from the original, as in the Igede rendering of Acts 18:6.

Before using a receptor-language idiom, check two things (ch. 4, Remember box):

- **Extra implications.** Does the idiom carry any meaning that does not fit the context?
- **Register.** Some idioms suit casual talk but not formal speech or Scripture read in worship.

Receptor-language idioms can also make a translation lively, even where the source has no idiom. Barnwell reports that Mbembe (Nigeria) renders Jesus' strict order in Mark 5:43 with an idiom about pulling ears (ch. 4.3).

## For the checker

1. What does this phrase mean here, in plain words?
2. If someone heard these words for the first time, what would they understand?
3. Does your language have an idiom with this same meaning? What else can that idiom mean?
4. Is this idiom suitable for Scripture read aloud in church, or is it too casual?
5. Did you keep a picture from the original that your readers will not understand?
6. Could this phrase be heard as an insult, a joke or a wrong meaning in your language?
