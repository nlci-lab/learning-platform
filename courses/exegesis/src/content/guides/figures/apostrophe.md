## What it is

**Apostrophe** is a figure of speech in which a speaker turns aside to speak directly to someone or something. The one addressed is often absent, or is a thing that cannot hear, such as a city, gates or death. The address can interrupt a speech or open it (Barnwell, ch. 34.8).

Speakers use apostrophe for poetic and dramatic effect. It catches attention and raises the feeling of the passage (Barnwell, ch. 34.8, 34.10).

When the speaker addresses a thing, apostrophe also involves [personification](~/learn/figures/personification/), because the thing is spoken to as if it were a person (Barnwell, ch. 34.8).

Do not mix this up with the punctuation mark called an apostrophe ('). That is a different meaning of the same word.

## How to spot it

- A calling word: older English versions use "O", as in "O Jerusalem".
- A name or noun used to call someone: "Death, where is ...", "Gates, lift up ...".
- A sudden change from "he" or "it" to "you" in the middle of a passage.
- Commands or questions given to something that cannot obey or answer.
- In a back translation, check that the reader can still tell who "you" is.

## Everyday examples

- A mourner at a grave says, "Father, why did you leave us so soon?"
- A tired traveller says, "Come on, old bus, just one more hill!"
- A poet writes, "Sea, give back the fishermen you took."

Every language has its own ways of calling someone. A translator can notice how people in their language speak to the dead, to animals or to objects in songs and laments. Those patterns may suit biblical apostrophe.

## Bible examples

**Psalm 24:7.** "Lift up your heads, O ye gates" (KJV). The psalmist commands the city gates to open for the King of glory. Gates cannot hear, so this is apostrophe with personification (Barnwell, ch. 34.8). The checker should ask what readers picture. "Lift up your heads" may need help if gates have no "heads" in the language.

**Luke 13:34.** "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, which killest the prophets" (KJV). Jesus speaks to the city as if it were present, and the city stands for its people. The checker should notice the double figure: apostrophe and the city for its people ([metonymy](~/learn/figures/metonymy/)). Readers need to know that Jesus is grieving over the people.

**1 Corinthians 15:55.** "O death, where is thy sting?" (KJV). Paul speaks to death as an enemy. Barnwell notes that apostrophe here is combined with [rhetorical questions](~/learn/figures/rhetorical-questions/) (ch. 34.8). The meaning is that death has lost its power. The checker should see that readers do not expect an answer about where the sting is.

**Jeremiah 46:9.** The prophet gives commands to horses and chariots, calling them to charge into battle (Barnwell, ch. 34.8). The address adds drama to the picture of war. Readers should understand it as a call to the army, not a talk with animals.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Apostrophe | The speaker turns to address someone or something | "O grave, where is your victory?" |
| [Personification](~/learn/figures/personification/) | A thing acts like a person, but no one speaks to it | "The grave opened its mouth." |
| [Rhetorical question](~/learn/figures/rhetorical-questions/) | A question that expects no answer | "Who can stand before him?" |

Apostrophe often occurs together with these figures. Name each one, then check that the translation keeps all of them clear.

## How translations handle it

- **Keep the direct address** when the language allows people to speak to absent people or to things, especially in poetry or laments.
- **Use the language's own calling form** in place of "O". Some modern English versions drop "O" and use only the name.
- **Mark the change of addressee** so readers know who "you" is.
- **Change to statements** if speaking to things is not natural. "Death has lost its power" in place of a question to death.
- **Name the real addressee** if a city or nation stands for its people.

Barnwell's general note on figures applies: first find the meaning, purpose and feeling of the figure, then find a natural way to give the same effect (ch. 34.9).

## For the checker

1. Who is speaking here, and to whom?
2. Is the one addressed present? Can it hear? How do readers understand that?
3. Where does the address start and stop? Can readers tell who "you" is?
4. How does your language speak to the dead, to a city or to things in songs?
5. Does this passage also use personification or a rhetorical question? Is each one clear?
6. Does the translation keep the strong feeling of the original?
