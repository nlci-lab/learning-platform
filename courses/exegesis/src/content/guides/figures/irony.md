## What it is

**Irony** is a [figure of speech](~/glossary/#figure-of-speech). The speaker openly seems to adopt another person's point of view, but in fact holds the opposite opinion (Barnwell, ch. 34.5). The words, taken at face value, say one thing. The speaker means something quite different, often the opposite.

People use irony to show an attitude. Often the attitude is disapproval or mockery. Irony can also make a point more strongly than a plain statement. Barnwell lists showing the speaker's attitude, catching attention and stirring an emotional response among the purposes of figures of speech (ch. 34.1).

Irony and [sarcasm](~/learn/figures/sarcasm/) are close. Barnwell treats them together and says both often involve saying the opposite of what is meant, with a strong attitude (ch. 34, summary).

## How to spot it

- **The words do not fit the facts.** The speaker praises something the context shows to be bad.
- **The speaker repeats an opponent's claim.** The speaker seems to agree with a view he clearly rejects.
- **Exclamations.** English translations often mark irony with an exclamation mark. It tells the reader to use the right tone when reading aloud (ch. 34.5).
- **Words used against their plain sense.** Words of praise, such as "glorious", "wise" or "rich", appear where the story shows shame, foolishness or need.
- **The narrator or the hearers.** The narrator may say the speaker "mocked" someone, or the hearers react with anger.

## Everyday examples

1. It rains all day at the picnic. Someone says, "What perfect weather for a picnic!"
2. A boy breaks three cups. His sister says, "You are so careful."
3. A man arrives two hours late. His friend says, "Thank you for coming so early."
4. A student fails every test. A classmate says, "Well, you are the expert, of course."

In each case the hearer knows the facts. That is why the words are heard as meaning the opposite.

## Bible examples

**1 Kings 18:27.** "Cry aloud: for he is a god" (KJV). The NIV has "Shout louder!" and "Surely he is a god!" Elijah does not believe Baal is a god. He speaks as if he accepts the prophets' view, then suggests Baal may be busy, away on a journey or asleep. The narrator says Elijah mocked them. *The checker should notice:* a literal translation could make Elijah seem to confess that Baal is a god.

**2 Samuel 6:20.** Michal says, "How glorious was the king of Israel to day" (KJV). She means the opposite. In her view David shamed himself by uncovering himself in front of servant girls (Barnwell, ch. 34.5). *The checker should notice:* hearers must not think Michal is praising David.

**1 Corinthians 4:8.** "Now ye are full, now ye are rich" (KJV). Paul seems to agree with the Corinthians' high view of themselves. The context shows he is correcting their pride (Barnwell lists this verse, ch. 34.5). *The checker should notice:* the next verses, where Paul describes the apostles as weak and poor, show the true meaning.

**2 Corinthians 12:13.** "Forgive me this wrong!" (NIV). Paul has just said that his only "fault" was not being a burden to them. He does not really ask forgiveness. He shows how unfair their complaint is (Barnwell lists this verse, ch. 34.5). *The checker should notice:* a back translation that reads like a sincere apology has lost the irony.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | What the speaker does | Main purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Irony | Seems to take another's view while holding the opposite | Show attitude, expose a wrong view |
| [Sarcasm](~/learn/figures/sarcasm/) | Expresses strong feeling, often taunting or insulting; may say the opposite of what is meant | Rebuke or ridicule |
| [Hyperbole](~/learn/figures/hyperbole/) | Says more than is literally true | Emphasis, drama |

Irony and sarcasm overlap, and Barnwell discusses them together (ch. 34.5). Sarcasm is usually sharper and aims to hurt. Hyperbole exaggerates in the same direction as the truth. Irony points in the opposite direction. A [rhetorical question](~/learn/figures/rhetorical-questions/) can also carry irony, as the next section shows.

## How translations handle it

Barnwell says each language has its own grammatical and word signals for irony. English uses words such as "fine" in an unexpected place, and the exclamation mark (ch. 34.5). The translator must use the signals of the receptor language, so that hearers know not to take the words literally. These signals are different in each language.

The main options are:

- **Keep the irony** and add the receptor language's own irony markers, such as particles, tone words or a fixed phrase.
- **Use a rhetorical question.** Barnwell reports that in Doohyayo (Cameroon) irony may be expressed by a rhetorical question (ch. 35.4).
- **Say the meaning more directly**, but keep the force and the attitude (ch. 34.5).

Barnwell asks readers to compare versions of Job 26:2–4, where Job answers his friends. A version that follows the form closely keeps the "How you have helped..." exclamations. The Message, as Barnwell reports, rewrites the passage in modern ironic English, with words such as "certainly" and "wonderful" (ch. 34.5). Both aim at the same meaning with different signals.

Whatever the choice, the translation should keep the attitude of the speaker. Meaning includes the speaker's attitude and the response aroused in the hearer (ch. 34.9).

## For the checker

1. Who is speaking here, and what do they really think about this? How do you know?
2. When people in your language hear this sentence, will they think the speaker means it literally?
3. How do people in your language show that they mean the opposite of their words? Did you use that here?
4. Please read this verse aloud as you would say it. Does the tone show the speaker's attitude?
5. If we said the meaning directly, would the force and the feeling stay the same?
6. Could anyone understand this as the speaker agreeing with the wrong view?
