## What it is

A [metonymy](~/glossary/#metonymy) uses the name of one thing to refer to another thing that is closely connected with it. In English, "the kettle is boiling" means the water in the kettle is boiling. The word "kettle" stands for its contents.

The connection can be of many kinds: a container for what it holds, a tool for the action done with it, a writer for what he wrote, a place for the people there, or a sign for the thing it stands for. Barnwell groups metonymy with [synecdoche](~/learn/figures/synecdoche/), because both use one idea to refer to a related idea (Barnwell, ch. 34.6).

Metonymy involves no comparison. That is the main difference from [metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/).

## How to spot it

- The literal meaning does not fit the sentence. A city cannot be built "with blood". A man cannot be "read".
- An object stands where you expect an action or a result: "sword" where the text means fighting, "throne" where it means royal rule.
- A person's name stands for his writings or teaching.
- A body part or object stands for a power or activity, and the verb fits the activity better than the object.
- Ask: "Is something being compared here?" If no comparison makes sense, but the two ideas are linked in real life, it is probably metonymy.

## Everyday examples

- "The office called me this morning." The office means a person who works there.
- "She has a good ear for music." The ear means the ability to hear and judge sound.
- "He reads Shakespeare every evening." Shakespeare means the plays he wrote.
- "The crown has signed the law." The crown means the king or queen, or the government.

## Bible examples

**Luke 1:32.** "The Lord God shall give unto him the throne of his father David" (KJV). The NIV keeps the same picture. The throne stands for royal rule, the right to reign as king. The checker should notice whether readers think only of a chair.

**Matthew 10:34.** "I came not to send peace, but a sword" (KJV). The sword stands for division and conflict, which the following verses describe within families. The checker should notice whether the translation suggests that Jesus brings weapons or war.

**Matthew 27:4.** Judas says he has "betrayed innocent blood" (NIV). Blood stands for the life of a person, here an innocent person who will be killed. A word-for-word rendering may make no sense, or may suggest that blood was literally handed over.

**Acts 15:21.** "Moses of old time hath in every city them that preach him" (KJV). Moses stands for the law that Moses wrote. Barnwell lists this verse among his metonymy exercises (ch. 34.6, exercise 7). Some versions make "the law of Moses" explicit.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | What links the two ideas | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Metonymy | A real-life association (container, tool, author, sign) | "sword" for conflict |
| [Synecdoche](~/learn/figures/synecdoche/) | Part and whole | "tongue" for the whole person |
| [Metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/) | A comparison: a point of similarity | "You are the salt of the earth" |
| [Idiom](~/learn/figures/idioms/) | A fixed phrase whose meaning is not the sum of its words | "hand of the Lord" used as a set phrase |

Barnwell notes that metonymy and synecdoche can work as idioms when the form is in common use (ch. 34.6). The line between metonymy and synecdoche is not always sharp. The name matters less than the meaning.

## How translations handle it

Barnwell says the translator must understand and communicate the actual meaning. He warns that a word-for-word rendering of these figures can be nonsense in the receptor language (ch. 34.6). The common options:

- **Keep the figure** if the receptor language uses the same association with the same meaning. "Throne" for rule may work in a language with kings.
- **Say the meaning plainly:** "will make him king like his ancestor David"; "I have come to bring division".
- **Use a receptor-language figure** that stands for the same thing in that culture.
- **Keep the word and add the meaning:** "the law that Moses wrote".
- **Add a footnote** when the image matters, for example a key term repeated across a book.

The KJV usually keeps the figure. The NIV keeps many figures too, but sometimes makes the meaning explicit, as it appears to do in Acts 15:21.

## For the checker

1. In this verse, what does the word "throne" (or "sword", "blood") stand for?
2. When people hear this sentence, what do they picture? Ask a reader who has not seen the source text.
3. Does your language use this thing to stand for the same idea? Can you give another example from daily speech?
4. If you kept the figure, does anything in the context make the meaning clear?
5. If you said the meaning plainly, did you keep the force of the original, or did it become weak?
6. Does the back translation show a picture or a plain meaning here? Is that what the team intended?
