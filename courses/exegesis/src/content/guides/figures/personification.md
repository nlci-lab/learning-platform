## What it is

[Personification](~/glossary/#personification) speaks of an idea, or something that is not alive, as if it were a person. The idea thinks, speaks, acts or feels. "Fear knocked at the door" makes fear a visitor.

Writers use personification to put focus on an idea or for dramatic effect (Barnwell, ch. 34.7). Barnwell also notes that it is not natural in every language or every context, so the form may need to change.

## How to spot it

- An abstract noun is the subject of an action verb: "faith saved you", "wisdom calls out", "sin reigns".
- A thing that is not alive does what people do: mountains sing, trees clap, the sea sees and runs away.
- Body parts or feelings are given to things: "the hand of the law", "the earth mourns".
- A thing is spoken to directly. That is [apostrophe](~/learn/figures/apostrophe/), which often includes personification.
- In a back translation, look for nouns like "faith", "grace" or "death" as the doer. Ask whether the language would say it that way.

## Everyday examples

- "Opportunity knocks only once."
- "The wind whispered in the trees."
- "Hunger drove him to steal."
- "The city never sleeps."

In some languages these sound natural. In others they sound odd, or readers may think the speaker believes the wind is a spirit. A translator can listen for whether people in their own language ever make ideas or objects act. That shows whether the figure can stay.

## Bible examples

**Mark 5:34.** "Thy faith hath made thee whole" (KJV). Faith is spoken of as the one who healed. The meaning is that the woman was healed because she believed. Barnwell reports that the CEV reorders this so that she is well because of her faith, and he suggests forms with a verb such as "because you believed" (ch. 34.7). The checker should notice who did the healing in the translation. A wrong form could suggest faith is a power of its own.

**Psalm 23:6.** "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life" (KJV). Goodness and mercy are spoken of as companions who follow. The meaning is that God will be good and kind to the speaker all through life. Barnwell lists this verse as one to re-express where personification is not possible (ch. 34.7).

**Isaiah 55:12.** "All the trees of the field shall clap their hands" (KJV). This is poetry. Creation is pictured as joining in joy. Readers should not think the trees will grow hands. The team may keep the picture if poetry in their language allows it, or show it is a picture.

**Luke 7:35.** "But wisdom is justified of all her children" (KJV). Wisdom is a mother with children. Barnwell reports that the GNT speaks of God's wisdom shown to be true by all who accept it (ch. 34.7). The checker should ask what "wisdom's children" means to readers.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Personification | A thing or idea acts like a person | "Mercy will follow me." |
| [Apostrophe](~/learn/figures/apostrophe/) | The speaker turns and addresses someone or something | "O death, where is your sting?" |
| [Metaphor](~/learn/figures/metaphor/) | One thing is called another | "The Lord is my shepherd." |
| [Metonymy](~/learn/figures/metonymy/) | A related word stands for the thing | "The whole city came out." |

Personification is a kind of figurative comparison: the idea is treated like a person. But no single person is named as the image.

## How translations handle it

- **Keep the figure** when it is natural in the language, especially in poetry.
- **Name the real actor.** Turn the abstract noun into a verb with a person as subject: "you are healed because you believed" (Barnwell, ch. 34.7).
- **State the meaning** in plain words. Barnwell reports that the GNT renders Isaiah 24:23, where the moon and sun are ashamed, as the moon growing dark and the sun no longer shining.
- **Show it is a picture** with a word such as "as if", where the language has one.

When you remove the figure, keep its emphasis. The original often uses personification to make an idea stand out.

## For the checker

1. Who or what is doing the action in this sentence?
2. Would people in your language say that this thing or idea can do this?
3. What do readers understand? Do they think the thing is alive or is a spirit?
4. If you changed the form, who is now the doer? Is that the right person?
5. Is the passage poetry? Can your language keep the picture in poetry?
6. Did the change lose any emphasis or feeling that the original had?
