## What it is

**Sarcasm** is a [figure of speech](~/glossary/#figure-of-speech) in which the speaker expresses strong feeling, often by taunting or insulting the hearer. Sometimes the speaker says one thing and means the opposite. Sarcasm is used to rebuke or to ridicule (Barnwell, ch. 34.5).

Sarcasm is close to [irony](~/learn/figures/irony/), and Barnwell discusses them together. In both, the plain sense of the words is not the meaning. Sarcasm is usually sharper. Its aim is to hurt, to shame or to correct.

## How to spot it

- **Praise words in a context of blame.** Words like "fine", "wonderful" or "clever" appear while the speaker is clearly angry.
- **The narrator names the attitude.** Words such as "mocked", "taunted" or "reviled" tell us how the words were meant.
- **Exclamations.** English translations often use an exclamation mark to show the tone (ch. 34.5).
- **Conflict.** Sarcasm often comes in a dispute or a scene of mockery.
- **An impossible claim.** The speaker gives someone a title or a quality that the speaker clearly denies.

## Everyday examples

1. A driver cuts in front of you. You say, "Very kind of you!"
2. A friend forgets your birthday again. You say, "You always remember the important things."
3. A team loses ten to nil. A fan says, "Great game, champions."
4. Someone gives advice that made things worse. You say, "Thanks, that really helped."

## Bible examples

**Mark 7:9.** "You have a fine way of setting aside the commands of God in order to observe your own traditions!" (NIV). The KJV has "Full well ye reject the commandment of God." Jesus does not praise the Pharisees and scribes. He rebukes them strongly (Barnwell, ch. 34.5). *The checker should notice:* a literal "you do it well" may sound like real praise in the receptor language.

**Mark 15:18.** "Hail, King of the Jews!" (KJV). The soldiers do not believe Jesus is a king. They kneel and greet him while they strike him and spit on him. Their words mock him (Barnwell lists Mark 15:18–20, ch. 34.5). *The checker should notice:* the greeting must sound like mockery, not respect. The surrounding actions help.

**Mark 15:31.** "He saved others; himself he cannot save" (KJV). The NIV has "He saved others," they said, "but he can't save himself!" The chief priests ridicule Jesus, then challenge him to come down from the cross (Barnwell lists Mark 15:31–32, ch. 34.5). *The checker should notice:* the narrator says they mocked him. The translation should keep that tone.

**Job 26:2–4.** Job answers his friend with a series of exclamations about how much he has "helped" the weak and "advised" the foolish. He means the friend has not helped at all. Barnwell asks readers to look for the word and grammar signals that show Job's attitude (ch. 34.5). *The checker should notice:* this is a long passage of sarcasm. Each line needs the same tone.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | Feeling | Aimed at | Typical cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sarcasm | Strong, often hostile | A person, to rebuke or ridicule | "A fine way of..." |
| [Irony](~/learn/figures/irony/) | Can be mild | A view or claim | Seeming to agree with an opponent |
| [Hyperbole](~/learn/figures/hyperbole/) | Any | Emphasis | Saying more than is literally true |

Sarcasm can use hyperbole, and it often uses irony. The question for translation is the same: what did the speaker really mean, and what attitude did he show?

## How translations handle it

Barnwell says each language has its own signals for sarcasm and irony. In English, the word "fine" in Mark 7:9 shows the speaker is being ironic. The exclamation mark also tells the reader how to say it (ch. 34.5). The translator must find the receptor language's own signals.

The options include:

- **Keep the form** and use receptor-language signals of sarcasm.
- **Say it more directly but with the same force.** Barnwell suggests that Mark 7:9 could be expressed as a strong statement that what they do is terrible (ch. 34.5).
- **Use a rhetorical question**, such as asking whether it is good to reject God's command for the sake of tradition (ch. 34.5). In Doohyayo (Cameroon), as Barnwell reports, the verse was translated as a question ending in a tag like "don't you?" (ch. 35.4).

Compare the English versions of Mark 7:9. The KJV "Full well" and the NIV "a fine way" both use words of praise to blame.

Meaning includes the attitude of the speaker and the response of the hearer (ch. 34.9). A translation that keeps the words but loses the anger has changed the meaning.

## For the checker

1. Is the speaker praising or rebuking here? How do you know from the context?
2. How do people in your language mock or rebuke someone using words of praise? Is that pattern used here?
3. Read the verse aloud. Does it sound angry or mocking, as it should?
4. Could someone hearing this think the speaker really means the kind words?
5. Would a direct rebuke or a question make the meaning clearer in your language?
6. Does the back translation still show the speaker's strong feeling?
