## First find the meaning

No strategy works until the meaning is clear. For every figure, know three things before you judge the draft:

- **what it means**, as the first hearers understood it;
- **what it does**: emphasis, feeling, a picture to remember, politeness;
- **how important it is** in the passage: a passing phrase, or the centre of the teaching.

Then choose the way of translating that keeps as much of that as the receptor language allows.

## Six ways to translate a figure

1. **Keep the figure as it is.** Use this when readers will understand it in the same way. It keeps the picture and the style.
2. **Keep the picture, but show it is a comparison.** A metaphor can become a simile: "Dan will be **like** a snake by the road" instead of "Dan will be a snake". Readers then know it is not literal.
3. **Keep the picture, and add the point.** Say what the two things share: "white **as snow**" is clear in some places; elsewhere "very white, like snow" or simply the point, "pure white", may be needed.
4. **Use a different figure from the receptor language** that has the same meaning and the same force. This often gives the most natural result, for example with idioms and euphemisms.
5. **Say the meaning plainly.** Drop the figure and state what it means. This is clear, but the picture and some of the force are lost, so make up for the force in another way where you can.
6. **Keep the figure and explain it outside the text,** in a footnote or the glossary. Useful when the picture matters, for example because it is echoed elsewhere in the Bible, but readers need help to understand it.

A team may use different ways for the same figure in different places. What matters is that each place communicates the meaning with the right force.

## How to choose

- **Is the picture important?** Keep it when it carries the teaching (the shepherd, the vine, the lamb) or links to other passages. Make the comparison or the point explicit if needed.
- **Is it unknown to readers?** If the picture is something readers do not know, the point may need to be stated. See [Translating unknown concepts](~/glossary/#unknown-concept).
- **Could it give a wrong meaning?** If the literal picture means something else, or something offensive, in the receptor culture, change it.
- **Is the force kept?** Hyperbole, litotes, irony and rhetorical questions exist for emphasis or feeling. If you drop the figure, keep the emphasis by other means.
- **Is it natural?** Some languages use few figures in certain kinds of text; others use many. Follow the patterns of the receptor language. Sometimes a figure fits in the translation where the source had none.

## Do not over-explain

Making the point of a figure explicit is not always best:

- it can make a vivid saying flat and long;
- many figures have more than one point, and stating one can narrow the meaning;
- some figures were meant to be puzzling to the first hearers. When Jesus warned about "the leaven of the Pharisees", the disciples did not understand at first. A translation should leave readers to puzzle too.

## How versions differ

Close (form-based) versions, such as the KJV and the NIV in many places, tend to **keep** figures as they are. Meaning-based versions, such as the GNT, NLT and CEV, more often **make the comparison or the point explicit, or say the meaning plainly**. Neither is simply right. Comparing them shows the options:

- When most versions keep a figure, it is probably clear in English. That does not mean it is clear in your language.
- When meaning-based versions explain a figure, read their explanation to check your own understanding of the meaning.
- When versions disagree about the meaning, use the method in [Weighing interpretations](~/guides/weighing-interpretations/).

## For the checker

- Does the team know this is a figure? How do they understand it?
- How did they translate it, and why that way?
- Ask someone who did not translate it: "What does this mean?" Does the answer match the meaning of the original?
- If the figure was dropped, is its emphasis or feeling still there?
- If a picture was replaced, does the new one fit the culture of the Bible and the context, and could it mislead?
- Is the same figure handled consistently where it is used elsewhere in the Bible?
