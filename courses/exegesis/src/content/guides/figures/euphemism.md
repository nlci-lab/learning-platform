## What it is

A [euphemism](~/glossary/#euphemism) is a mild or indirect way of saying something. Speakers use it when the direct words would sound harsh, rude, shameful or frightening. They also use it when something is too holy to name directly (Barnwell, ch. 34.2).

Barnwell calls these two kinds positive and negative. A positive euphemism shows respect, as when Jewish speakers said "heaven" for God. A negative euphemism avoids something unpleasant, such as death, sex or bodily functions.

Every culture has euphemisms, but they differ. What one culture says directly, another may only say indirectly.

## How to spot it

- The topic is death, sex, the body, illness or God, but the words are soft or vague.
- A common verb has an unusual object: "knew his wife", "went in to her".
- A word for sleep, rest or going away is used about someone who has died.
- A place or quality is named where you expect the name of God: "heaven", "the Most High", "the Mighty One".
- The literal meaning seems too small or odd for what happens next in the story.

## Everyday examples

- "Her grandfather passed away last year." He died.
- "He needs to use the bathroom." He needs to pass urine or stool.
- "They are expecting a baby." She is pregnant.
- "The company let him go." He lost his job.

## Bible examples

**Acts 13:36.** David "fell on sleep, and was laid unto his fathers" (KJV). Both phrases mean that he died and was buried. The checker should notice whether readers think David only took a rest.

**Luke 15:18.** The lost son plans to say, "I have sinned against heaven, and before thee" (KJV). "Heaven" stands for God, out of respect. Barnwell reports that the GNT says "God" directly here (ch. 34.2). The checker should notice whether "heaven" in the receptor language could mean only the sky.

**Genesis 4:1.** "Adam knew Eve his wife" (KJV). This means they had sexual relations, and the next words say that she conceived. Barnwell reports that the NIV says Adam "made love to" Eve, and the GNT uses a direct term (ch. 34.2). The checker should notice whether "knew" in the receptor language only means "was acquainted with".

**Judges 3:24.** The king's servants find the doors locked and think he is relieving himself. The Hebrew uses an indirect phrase. Barnwell lists this verse with English bathroom euphemisms (ch. 34.2). Older and newer English versions handle the phrase in different ways, so the checker should ask what the team's wording means to its readers.

## Not to be confused with

| Figure | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Euphemism | Soften, or show respect | "fell asleep" for died |
| [Idiom](~/learn/figures/idioms/) | Fixed phrase with a special meaning, not always polite | "kick the bucket" for died |
| [Litotes](~/learn/figures/litotes/) | Emphasis by denying the opposite | "no small stir" |
| [Metonymy](~/learn/figures/metonymy/) | Name a connected thing | "throne" for rule |

Many euphemisms are also idioms. "Kick the bucket" and "pass away" are both idioms about death, but only the second is polite. A euphemism is defined by its purpose: to soften or to show respect. Some euphemisms are also metonymies, as "heaven" for God is.

## How translations handle it

First, the translator must recognise the euphemism and understand the actual meaning. Then Barnwell gives three options, based on what is acceptable in the receptor culture (ch. 34.2):

- **Use a direct form** where the receptor culture speaks plainly about this topic.
- **Use a different euphemism** from the receptor language that has the same meaning there.
- **Add a euphemism** where the source text is direct, if direct words would offend the receptor culture.

Barnwell warns that a word-for-word euphemism can be meaningless, give a wrong meaning, or even offend (ch. 34.2). A footnote can explain a source euphemism that the team chooses to keep.

Versions differ. The KJV keeps the Hebrew and Greek euphemisms. The NIV often uses a modern English euphemism. The GNT, as Barnwell reports, often says the meaning directly.

## For the checker

1. What is this phrase really talking about? Can the team say it plainly to you?
2. When people in your community hear this phrase, what will they understand?
3. How do people in your language talk about this topic politely? Could it be read aloud in church without shame?
4. Is there a verse here where the source text is direct, but your community would expect softer words?
5. If the team used a euphemism, is its meaning still clear to young people and to people from other areas?
6. Does the back translation show a plain meaning or a polite one? Is that what the team intended?
